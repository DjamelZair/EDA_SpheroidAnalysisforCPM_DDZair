Unified tabular learning

A shared 64-dimensional latent space across heterogeneous tabular datasets.

A self-supervised autoencoder learns a shared 64-d latent space across three heterogeneous tabular datasets (Covertype, HIGGS, HELOC). A multi-head MLP and an XGBoost trained on the latents reach ROC-AUC 0.811, beating the TabPFN-2.5 baseline at 0.660. Scales past 50,000 samples where TabPFN starts to degrade, on a single GPU-free machine.

How it works

Per-stage breakdown
Stage 01

Three datasets

Covertype (forest cover), HIGGS (particle physics), HELOC (credit risk). Heterogeneous schemas, sizes, and class balance.

Stage 02

Pre-processing

Per-dataset standardisation, categorical encoding, alignment of feature dimensions for the shared encoder input.

Stage 03 · Highlight

Shared autoencoder

Self-supervised reconstruction objective on a shared 64-d latent space. The encoder is dataset-agnostic.

Stage 04

Multi-head MLP

Per-dataset classification heads on top of the shared latent. Joint loss over three heads.

Stage 05

XGBoost on latents

The 64-d latent vectors fed into XGBoost. Best of both worlds: representation learning plus tree boosting.

Stage 06

Baselines

Single-head MLP, multi-head MLP, XGBoost on latents, TabPFN-2.5. All compared on the same held-out folds.

Stage 07

Diagnostics

t-SNE on latents, latent correlation matrices, per-dataset confusion matrices and ROC curves.

Stage 08

Output

ROC-AUC 0.811 (XGB on latents), surpassing TabPFN-2.5 at 0.660. Scales smoothly past 50,000 samples.

The comparison

ROC-AUC across heads

The latent space carries the signal better than the raw data.

XGBoost trained on the 64-d shared latent reaches 0.811 ROC-AUC, against the single-head MLP at 0.703 and the multi-head MLP at 0.793. The TabPFN-2.5 baseline sits at 0.660 and does not scale past ~10k samples; the shared autoencoder approach scales cleanly past 50k.

The win comes from compressing heterogeneous tabular data into a representation that downstream classifiers can exploit, without per-dataset feature engineering.

A shared latent space plus a tree booster beats a transformer baseline on heterogeneous tabular data.

Inside the latent space

how it actually looks

What this picture means

The shared autoencoder maps every row of every dataset (Higgs particles, HELOC credit decisions, Covertype forest pixels) into the same 64-dimensional space. That's invisible. To see it, t-SNE compresses those 64 numbers down to two, preserving local distances, so similar rows end up near each other. Each colour is a class label; well-separated colours mean the network learned a representation where classes are linearly distinguishable.

What to look for in the figure on the right: three large clusters (one per dataset), with within-cluster colour separation. Higgs (binary) shows a clean two-cluster split; HELOC (binary) is tighter; Covertype (7 classes) splits into multiple bands.
